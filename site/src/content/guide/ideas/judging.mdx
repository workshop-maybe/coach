You judge every result yourself. The app has no AI inside deciding for you. It puts the reply beside the rubric and asks one thing: <UI>Did it meet it</UI>.

## Read before you decide

Replies arrive faster than anyone reads them, and a confident wrong answer looks just like a right one. So read the whole reply against the rubric, one point at a time. Check what it cites: look up the files, commits and facts it names. One invented piece of evidence is enough to fail it.

Taking that time is the point. A score on someone else's test can't tell you whether an agent does your work. Your rubric, applied by you, can.

<UI>Show as stored</UI> shows the rubric and the reply as the Markdown they are stored as. <UI>Show formatted</UI> goes back.

## Met or not yet

Choose <UI>Met</UI> when the reply meets the rubric as written, and <UI>Not yet</UI> when it doesn't. The app saves your verdict with the reply as it stands in the reply box and the handoff you copied. [Judge a reply](/how-to/judge-a-reply/) has the steps.

Not yet isn't a final word. You can test the same agent again, and every result is kept. Nothing is overwritten, so an agent can have several results on one badge, and the latest one counts. [Compare takes](/how-to/compare-takes/) shows how to read any two side by side.

## A second opinion, only if you ask

With [Jev Mode](/help/jev-mode/) on, the judge card offers <UI>Ask Jev</UI>. Jev, TypeSafe's model, reads the reply against the rubric and the badge's learning targets, and calls each one: <UI>Meets</UI>, <UI>Misses</UI> or <UI>Can't call</UI>. A point it can't call either way says <UI>Can't call: sharpen this point</UI>.

A call says more than a percentage. A 90% chance is enough to say a point is met, but a bare "90%" reads as doubt. So the app turns each chance into a call, and keeps the chance beside it in small type.

Jev's reading doesn't decide anything. You still choose <UI>Met</UI> or <UI>Not yet</UI>, then <UI>Save</UI>, and the reading is kept with your result.

## Your verdicts teach the context

Jev learns nothing on its side. What changes is what the app sends it. Each time you ask, a few of your own earlier verdicts on the same badge go along as examples: replies you judged Met, replies that missed a point you tapped, and first of all replies where you went against Jev, with your note on why. Only the opening of each reply goes, and only replies on this badge, because unrelated text makes Jev's answers worse.

Your verdicts also set where Jev's calls fall. After eight judged readings on a badge, the cut-offs between meets, can't call and misses move to match how you judge there, and the reading shows how often Jev's call has matched yours. That number appears only after you pick, so it can't decide for you.
